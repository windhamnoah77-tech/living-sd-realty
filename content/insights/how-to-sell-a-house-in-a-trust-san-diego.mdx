sections:
  - h: "Can a trustee sell a house without going to court?"
    p: "Usually, yes. When a home is titled in a living trust, the successor trustee typically has authority under the trust document to sell it without probate. The title company and escrow still need proof of that authority, and your attorney should confirm that nothing in the trust limits or conditions the sale. If the home was never transferred into the trust, it may have to go through probate instead, which follows a different, court-supervised process."
  - h: "Step 1: Confirm your authority"
    p: "Before anything is listed, get the paperwork that proves you can sign."
    bullets:
      - "A copy of the trust and any amendments"
      - "A certified death certificate for the person who created the trust"
      - "A Certification of Trust (California Probate Code §18100.5), which title companies use instead of the full trust"
      - "An affidavit of death of trustee, usually recorded with the county to clear title"
      - "If there are co-trustees, who must sign and how decisions are made"
  - h: "Step 2: Notify beneficiaries and heirs"
    p: "California requires the trustee to send a formal notice to beneficiaries and heirs after the trust becomes irrevocable (Probate Code §16061.7). That notice starts a period in which someone could contest the trust. Many trustees prepare the home during this window and talk with their attorney about whether to list before it ends. Clear, early communication with beneficiaries prevents most disputes later."
  - h: "Step 3: Get a date-of-death value"
    p: "Inherited property generally receives a stepped-up tax basis to its value on the date of death. A written date-of-death appraisal supports that number for the CPA and the beneficiaries, and it gives you a defensible starting point for pricing. Talk with your CPA about taxes before you accept an offer."
  - h: "Step 4: Decide how much prep makes sense"
    p: "A trustee's job is to get a fair result for the beneficiaries, not to remodel. The right level of prep depends on the home and the budget."
    bullets:
      - "Clear out personal property, or arrange an estate sale first"
      - "Handle safety items and obvious deferred maintenance"
      - "Consider paint, cleaning and landscaping when the numbers support it"
      - "Get pre-listing inspections (including sewer lateral on older homes) to avoid surprises in escrow"
      - "Keep receipts: every expense should be documented for the trust accounting"
  - h: "Step 5: Disclosures for a home you may never have lived in"
    p: "Trustees are generally exempt from California's standard Transfer Disclosure Statement, but that does not mean no disclosures. You still provide the natural hazard disclosure and other required reports, and you must disclose material facts you know about. Inspections help fill in what you don't know. Confirm the exact package with your agent and attorney."
  - h: "Step 6: Price it so the decision holds up"
    p: "Beneficiaries will ask how the price was set. Use recent comparable sales, current competition and the property's condition, and keep a written record of the pricing rationale and every offer received. A price you can explain protects you if anyone questions the sale later."
  - h: "Step 7: Choose the offer, close, and account for it"
    p: "The strongest offer is not always the highest price. Weigh contingencies, financing and timeline, and document why you chose the offer you did. After closing, the proceeds go to the trust account, and your attorney and CPA guide the final accounting and distribution."
  - h: "How long does a trust sale take?"
    p: "Once authority is confirmed and the home is ready, a typical San Diego trust sale takes roughly 30–60 days on market and 30 days in escrow. The prep phase, including clean-out, notice period and repairs, is what varies most. Homes in probate take longer because of court steps."

faq:

    a: "Generally no, if the home was properly titled in the trust. If it was left outside the trust, probate or another court process may be required. Your attorney can confirm."

  - q: "Can one beneficiary stop the sale?"
    a: "The trustee usually holds the authority to sell, but a beneficiary can object or go to court. Clear communication and a well-documented price reduce that risk."
