sections:
  - h: "The simple definition"
    p: "A date-of-death appraisal (also called a time-of-death or retrospective appraisal) is a professional appraisal completed today with an effective valuation date in the past: the date the owner died. The appraiser reconstructs the market as it existed on that date and estimates what the property would have sold for then. It is not a Realtor CMA, a Zestimate or a guess. It is documentation, and for an inherited San Diego home it can influence taxes, estate administration, negotiations and how well beneficiaries get along."
  - h: "The fast answer: keep two values separate"
    p: "The cleanest estate files keep a date-of-death value and a current market value, and use each for its own job. Mixing them up confuses people, confusion turns emotional, and emotional decisions slow the sale and cost money."
    bullets:
      - "Date-of-death value: basis support and estate records. What was the property worth at the moment of death?"
      - "Current market value: pricing and selling today. What is it worth if we list it now?"
  - h: "Why this matters in San Diego"
    p: "San Diego is a collection of micro-markets. Two blocks can price differently, and school boundaries, views, noise, lot shape, coastal proximity and renovation quality all change which sales are truly comparable. When family members pull their own comps, they often pull the wrong ones, then question the trustee's price or argue for an unrealistic one. A professional retrospective appraisal reduces that risk."
  - h: "Who typically orders the appraisal"
    p: "Usually the trustee, the executor or court-appointed administrator, the estate attorney, the CPA supporting basis documentation, or heirs who want a neutral baseline. With multiple beneficiaries, ordering it early is a smart move: it anchors the conversation to documentation instead of opinions."
  - h: "The tax angle: step-up in basis"
    p: "Capital gains are measured from basis. When property is inherited, basis commonly steps up to fair market value at the date of death, and a date-of-death appraisal helps document that value. Simplified example: the owner bought for $200,000, the home was worth $950,000 at death, and the heirs later sell for $975,000. The gain in that scenario is much closer to $25,000 than $775,000. The bigger the appreciation, the riskier sloppy documentation becomes. This is general information, not tax advice; your attorney and CPA should guide your exact situation."
  - h: "Why “we guessed the value” is a trap"
    p: "Screenshotting Zillow, using a rough comp sheet, asking a friend or picking a number that feels right may never be challenged. But if the home sells years later, the file gets reviewed or beneficiaries disagree, you may not be able to defend that number. Document it instead of guessing."
  - h: "Benefits for trustees, executors and heirs"
    p: "A trust or estate seller is usually a fiduciary who should be able to show what they did and why."
    bullets:

      - "Reduces beneficiary conflict over value, strategy, timing, repairs and price reductions."
      - "Strengthens negotiation discipline, so pressure does not lead to bad concessions or panic price cuts."
      - "Creates a clean record of what you knew, what you decided and why, which supports prudence and reasonableness."
      - "Supports basis documentation even if the home is rented, held or distributed and sold years later."
  - h: "Benefits for buyers"

      - "A more rational negotiation, with less risk of beneficiary regret leading to renegotiation."
      - "A clear separation between value and condition, so repair requests can be tied to actual changes since the date of death."

  - h: "How a retrospective value is built"
    bullets:
      - "Confirm the effective date: the date of death."
      - "Document the property: location, lot, living area, beds and baths, parking, layout, views, noise, condition, updates and zoning or site influences."
      - "Reconstruct condition as of the date of death. Photos, repair invoices, inspection reports and prior listing photos all help, especially if the home has changed since."
      - "Select comparable sales close in time and location to the effective date, similar in size, utility and condition."
      - "Adjust for differences in size, lot, view, condition, upgrades, parking and market timing."
      - "Reconcile to a final value with readable, supported reasoning, not just a number."
  - h: "A two-track plan for trustees and heirs"
    p: "Run documentation and sale strategy side by side. Track one: get the date-of-death appraisal and use it for basis, the estate record and reducing conflict. Track two: build today's sale plan, deciding between as-is, light prep or selective improvements, then price and timeline. For most estates the middle path works best: deep clean, yard cleanup, safety items, paint or flooring only if truly needed, staging if the numbers support it, clear disclosures, easy access and clear pricing. Selective improvements should be rare, justified and time-boxed, never an open-ended remodel."
  - h: "Mistakes that blow up estate sales"
    bullets:
      - "No documentation, only opinions, so the loudest beneficiary becomes the pricing committee."
      - "Anchoring today's list price to the date-of-death value. Markets move."
      - "Pulling comps from the wrong pocket of the neighborhood."
      - "Over-repairing without a plan: money spent, timeline stretched, and a discount at the end anyway."
      - "Under-preparing and still expecting retail pricing."

faq:
  - q: "Is a date-of-death appraisal the same as a normal appraisal?"
    a: "It follows the same professional standards. The difference is the effective date: a normal appraisal values the property today, while a date-of-death appraisal values it as of a past date."
  - q: "Do I need one if I sell immediately?"
    a: "Often yes, because basis documentation is tied to the date of death, estate records should be clean and beneficiaries usually need a baseline. Ask your attorney or CPA for your situation."
  - q: "Can I use Zillow instead of an appraisal?"
    a: "Zillow is an automated estimate and can be wrong. A professional appraisal is generally much stronger documentation."
  - q: "What if the home changed or was remodeled after the date of death?"
    a: "A retrospective appraisal values the condition as of the date of death. Later changes affect today's value, not the historical value, so document the earlier condition as well as you can."
