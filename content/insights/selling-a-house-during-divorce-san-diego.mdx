sections:
  - h: "The house is often the hardest piece of a divorce"
    p: "For most couples the home is the largest asset they own together, and it carries memories, kids' school routines and a monthly payment that someone has to keep making. The good news is that the real estate side follows a fairly predictable path once you understand three things: what California law says about the house, which options you actually have, and how the court process affects timing. This guide walks through each in plain language. It is general information from a real estate agent, not legal or tax advice, so confirm your specific situation with a California family law attorney and a CPA."
  - h: "What California law says about the house"
    p: "California is a community property state. In general, property acquired during the marriage is community property and is divided equally, while property owned before the marriage, or received as a gift or inheritance, is usually separate property. A home bought before the marriage but paid down with marital income can be partly both, which is a question for your attorney. Until there is a court order dividing things, the California courts' self-help site puts it simply: property from the marriage still belongs to both of you."
    bullets:
      - "Automatic restraining orders: the divorce Summons (form FL-110) includes standard family law restraining orders. Among other things, they generally bar either spouse from selling, transferring or borrowing against community or separate property without the other spouse's written consent or a court order. They apply to the spouse who files once the case is filed, and to the other spouse once served."
      - "What that means in practice: once a case is open, a sale needs both spouses to agree in writing or a judge to approve it. Title and escrow companies will want both signatures, and often a copy of the stipulation or order."
      - "Check how title is held: many couples hold their home in a joint revocable living trust. That changes who signs and what title needs to see. Your attorney should confirm how the trust is handled in the divorce."
  - h: "Your three main options"
    p: "Nearly every divorcing owner ends up choosing one of these paths. The right one depends on equity, income, the kids and how well the two of you can work together."
    bullets:
      - "One spouse buys out the other: the staying spouse keeps the house and pays the other their share of the equity, usually by refinancing. This keeps stability for the family but only works if the staying spouse can qualify for a loan alone."
      - "Sell now and split the proceeds: the house is listed, sold on the open market and the net proceeds are divided as agreed or ordered. This is often the cleanest financial break and gives both people cash to start over."
      - "Keep it jointly for a set period, then sell: some couples agree, or a court orders, that the house is sold later, for example when the youngest child finishes the school year. This needs a clear written agreement about who pays the mortgage, taxes, insurance and repairs in the meantime."
  - h: "How a buyout works"
    p: "A buyout sounds simple, but there are several moving parts. Start by agreeing on value. A neutral appraisal, or a written opinion of value from an agent both of you trust, avoids arguing over numbers later."
    bullets:
      - "Equity math: current value minus the loan balance equals equity. If the house is community property, the leaving spouse's share is generally half of that, adjusted by anything else in the overall settlement."
      - "Refinance: the staying spouse usually refinances to pay off the joint loan and fund the payout. Signing a deed alone does not remove the leaving spouse from the mortgage; only paying off or refinancing the loan does."
      - "Qualifying: with San Diego prices, many single-income buyouts do not pencil. Talk to a lender early, before the settlement is drafted around a buyout that cannot close."
      - "The deed: the leaving spouse typically signs a deed transferring their interest to the staying spouse, prepared by your attorney or title company and recorded with the San Diego County Recorder."
  - h: "Who decides whether the house is sold?"
    p: "Ideally, the two of you do, with help from your attorneys or a mediator, and the decision is written into a stipulation or the final judgment. If you cannot agree, a judge can decide how the house is handled, including ordering a sale. If one spouse refuses to sign documents after a sale is ordered, a court can authorize someone else to sign on that spouse's behalf. That is slow and expensive, so most couples are better off agreeing on the rules of the sale up front."
  - h: "Timing the sale with the court process"
    p: "You can sell before, during or after the divorce is final. Each has trade-offs."
    bullets:
      - "Before or during the case: possible with both spouses' written agreement or a court order. Selling early stops the arguments about who pays the mortgage and gives both people cash for housing. The proceeds are often held until the property division is settled."
      - "After the judgment: the judgment usually spells out whether the house is sold, the deadline, who controls the listing and how proceeds are split. Title will ask for a copy, so read it with your attorney before listing."
      - "Market timing: in San Diego, spring tends to bring more buyers, but a well-priced home sells year round. A divorce deadline matters more than the season, so build in roughly 30 to 60 days on market plus about 30 days of escrow, and more if prep is needed."
  - h: "Splitting the proceeds"
    p: "At closing, escrow pays off the mortgage, any liens, closing costs and the agreed commissions. What remains is the net proceeds. How that money is divided depends on your agreement or court order, not on the escrow company."
    bullets:
      - "Holding the money: if the division is not final, the parties often agree to hold proceeds in a blocked account or an attorney's trust account until it is."
      - "Reimbursement claims: if one spouse kept paying the mortgage after separation, or one spouse lived in the house alone, there may be credits or charges that shift the split. In California these are sometimes called Epstein credits and Watts charges. Your attorney handles these."
      - "Capital gains: federal law lets a single filer exclude up to $250,000 of gain on a main home, or $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly, if ownership and use tests are met. IRS Publication 523 also says a transfer of the home to a spouse or ex-spouse as part of a divorce settlement is generally treated as having no gain or loss, and it has special rules when one spouse has moved out. Ask a CPA how these apply to you and to your California return."
  - h: "Keeping the sale civil"
    p: "The sale goes faster and nets more money when both owners agree on the process before the sign goes up. A few written ground rules prevent most fights."
    bullets:
      - "One agent who communicates with both of you equally, in writing, with the same updates going to both owners (and attorneys, if you want)."
      - "Agree on the list price and a price reduction rule in advance, for example: if no acceptable offer in 21 days, reduce by a set amount."
      - "Decide who handles showings, keys and the home's condition, especially if one spouse still lives there."
      - "Agree on a prep budget and how repair costs are split or reimbursed from proceeds."
      - "Set an offer rule: for example, both must respond within 24 hours, and any offer at or above an agreed number will be accepted."
      - "Consider whether a quieter, limited marketing approach fits your privacy needs, and weigh the trade-offs. See /insights/off-market-vs-mls-san-diego-sellers-investors."

faq:
  - q: "Can my spouse sell the house without my consent during a divorce in California?"
    a: "Generally no. Once a case is filed and served, the standard restraining orders on the Summons bar selling or transferring property without the other spouse's written consent or a court order. Confirm details with your attorney."
  - q: "Should we sell the house before or after the divorce is final?"
    a: "Either can work. Selling earlier can reduce conflict over payments and give both spouses cash, while selling after judgment lets the court order spell out every step. Your attorney can tell you which fits your case."
  - q: "Who pays the mortgage while the house is for sale?"
    a: "That is decided by agreement or court order. Keep paying on time either way, because a late payment hurts both of your credit scores, and any reimbursement can be sorted out from the proceeds."
  - q: "How do we agree on a list price if we don't trust each other?"
    a: "Use a neutral appraisal or a written market analysis with recent comparable sales that both of you receive at the same time, and agree on a price reduction rule before listing."
